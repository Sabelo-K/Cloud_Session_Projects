Penny buys lunch for twenty-four dollars. Her account is a few dollars short. The bank pays it anyway, and charges her thirty-four dollars for the favour. She pays it back three days later. If that were a loan, it would cost about seventeen thousand percent a year.
Last episode, pay later made small debts feel free. Today, the opposite. Here are three ways the system charges the most to the people who have the least.
Engine number one: overdraft. When an account dips below zero, many banks will cover the purchase and charge a fee. The government's consumer watchdog found the typical fee was thirty-four dollars, and most were triggered by purchases of twenty-four dollars or less. More than half of those negative balances were paid back within three days.
And it isn't spread evenly. Roughly eight percent of accounts generate most of the overdraft fees. People living closest to zero. Even after many banks cut these fees, Americans still paid more than five point eight billion dollars in overdraft and bounced-payment fees in twenty twenty-three.
And that's after the fees came down. Before the pandemic, big banks were collecting eleven to twelve billion dollars a year from these fees.
Engine number two: fast cash. When rent is due before payday, and there's no savings to fall back on, a payday loan can look like a lifeline. A typical one charges fifteen dollars for every hundred borrowed, due in two weeks. Fifteen percent doesn't sound outrageous.
But that's for just two weeks. Stretched across a year, it works out to almost four hundred percent. And if Penny can't repay it all on payday, she can pay another fee to roll it over, and the clock starts again. The loan was supposed to bridge the gap. Instead, the gap gets wider.
Engine number three: not having a bank at all. In twenty twenty-three, about five point six million American households had no bank account. The most common reason? They didn't have enough money to meet the minimum balance.
So the people with the least money end up paying to use their own. A fee to cash a paycheck. A fee to pay a bill. A fee to load a prepaid card. Each one is small. Together, they're a tax on being broke that people with a little cushion never pay.
The novelist Terry Pratchett had a name for this. The boots theory. A rich person buys one good pair of boots that lasts for years. A poor person can only afford cheap boots, which fall apart, so they keep buying them, and end up spending more and still have wet feet. It's fiction. But overdrafts, payday loans and check-cashing fees are the real-world version.
So here's the machine. Overdraft charges you for being a little short. Fast cash charges you for being short on time. And no bank charges you for being short, full stop. The cheapest thing you can own is a small cushion. One: turn off overdraft coverage, so a short balance means a declined card, not a thirty-four dollar fee. Two: look for a free checking account with no minimum balance. Three: start an emergency fund, even if it's just ten dollars a week. The first five hundred dollars does the most work.
