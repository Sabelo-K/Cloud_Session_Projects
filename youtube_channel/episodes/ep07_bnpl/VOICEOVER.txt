Penny wants a jacket that costs a hundred and thirty-five dollars. At checkout, a button offers to split it into four easy payments. No interest. No credit card. Just tap. So if Penny pays zero interest, how does the lender make any money?
Last episode, subscriptions charged Penny a little every month. Buy now, pay later looks like the opposite: a loan that costs nothing. Here are the three engines that make "free" credit a multi-billion-dollar business.
And it took off fast. In twenty nineteen, the five biggest lenders made about seventeen million of these loans. Two years later, it was a hundred and eighty million. Something that grows that fast is making somebody a lot of money.
Engine number one: the shop pays. When Penny uses pay later, the lender pays the shop right away, minus a cut. And that cut is bigger than a card's. In a government study of the biggest lenders, the shop's fee averaged about two and a half percent of each sale. The comparable card fee in that same study was under one percent.
So why would a shop pay more? Because people who can split the price tend to buy more, and buy bigger. The lender is selling the shop Penny's willingness to spend money she doesn't have yet.
Engine number two: late fees. The interest-free deal only works if every payment lands on time. Miss one, and many lenders charge a late fee, often around seven dollars. In twenty twenty-one, about one in ten borrowers was charged at least one.
And a missed payment can cost more than that fee. The lender usually takes payments automatically from Penny's debit card. If her account is empty, it can try again, and again. Regulators found some lenders retried a single payment up to eight times. Each failed attempt can trigger a fee at Penny's bank as well.
Engine number three: the habit. Each loan feels tiny. Thirty-three dollars here. Twenty dollars there. So it's easy to have several at once. In twenty twenty-two, sixty-three percent of borrowers took out more than one of these loans during the year, and a third used more than one lender.
The big lenders made two hundred and seventy-seven million of these loans in a single year, worth thirty-four billion dollars. And nearly two thirds of them went to people with lower credit scores. The customers most likely to stack payments are the ones with the least room for a slip.
Here's how it sneaks up. Each plan usually takes a payment every two weeks. One plan is easy to track. Four plans, from three different apps, can all land in the same week as rent. Nothing about any single loan is scary. It's the pile that hurts.
So here's the machine. The shop pays to get you spending. Late fees punish the slip. And the habit keeps you coming back. Pay later isn't bad on its own, but it's still debt, even when it feels like shopping. One: only use it for things you could buy today with cash. Two: never have more than one plan running at a time. Three: put every due date in your calendar, and keep the money in your account.
